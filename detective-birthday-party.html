How to throw a detective birthday party at home

A detective party is cheap, works in a small house and keeps a room full of kids busy for an hour. The best part is making the birthday child the lead detective on a case where their own family are the suspects.

The two-hour party plan

TimeWhat happens
0:00–0:20Detective check-in. Guests get an ID badge, take their fingerprints, and pick a detective name.
0:20–0:25The crime is discovered. A grown-up “finds” the empty cake plate and reads the mission letter aloud.
0:25–1:10The investigation. Teams hunt for clue cards, crack codes and cross off suspects.
1:10–1:20The accusation. Teams present their case. The thief reads their confession.
1:20–1:45Cake and celebration. The “stolen” cake is recovered just in time.
1:45–2:00Certificates and goody bags. Everyone gets promoted to detective.

1. Invitations

Make them look like case files: “You are summoned to investigate a mystery at 14 Palm Grove. Bring your sharpest eyes.” Ask guests to come in a detective disguise: hats, sunglasses and trench coats all count.

2. Arrival: detective check-in

  • Fingerprint station. A washable ink pad, index cards and a magnifying glass. Kids compare swirls, loops and arches.
  • Detective ID badges. Name, detective nickname and a thumbprint as the “photo”.
  • Sunglasses and moustaches. Cheap disguises make great photos.

3. The main event: a mystery starring the family

Sleuthhouse builds a printable case in about five minutes. Add the birthday child as lead detective, pick The Great Birthday Cake Caper, and add the suspects: parents, grandparents, the family dog and the birthday child’s favourite toy. The engine checks that the clues point to exactly one thief, so the investigation always has a fair, satisfying answer.

For a party, turn on party mode. Guests split into Team Red and Team Blue, each hiding spot gets a card for each team, and they race to crack the same case. Pairs or teams of three to five work best.

Picking the difficulty

  • Ages 5–7: Rookie Detective. Riddles, and every clue crosses someone off. Have one grown-up per team to read.
  • Ages 8–10: Junior Inspector. Emoji codes and a little logic. Teams can run it alone.
  • Ages 11+: Master Sleuth. Ciphers and a full logic grid. Surprisingly tough.

Tips from the case files

  • Hide clue cards before guests arrive, and keep the grown-ups’ sheet in your pocket for hints.
  • Keep one room off-limits (say “that room is not part of the investigation”) and put the real cake there.
  • If a team makes a wrong accusation, tell them the suspect has an alibi and send them back to their notebook.
  • Ask the “thief” to be a good sport and ham up the confession. It’s the moment kids remember.

4. Food ideas with a detective twist

  • “Evidence” cookies with a thumbprint of jam.
  • Magnifying-glass lollipops or round crackers with a pretzel-stick handle.
  • The recovered cake with a tiny “CASE CLOSED” flag on top.

5. Goody bags

Every kit includes a certificate and cut-out badges for each detective. Add a small notebook, a pencil, a plastic magnifying glass and some “secret code” stickers, and you’re done.

Questions

How many kids can play?

One to about ten. With more than three kids, use party mode so two teams race to solve the same case.

Can the birthday child’s friends be suspects?

Yes, you can add anyone’s name. Most families find it funnier to make the grown-ups and pets the suspects, so no guest feels picked on.

We live in a flat with only a few rooms. Does it still work?

Yes. Add the rooms you have, and use more hiding spots per room (under a cushion, inside a shoe, behind a picture frame).

How long should the party last?

About two hours works well: see the timeline above.
